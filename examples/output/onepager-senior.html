The Question

核心问题

Consumers who stop buying blind boxes may still compare their outcomes with a peer's success. We study how this persistent social comparison affects a firm's price and product design in a two-period model. The firm commits to a fixed price and a target-item probability. Under the maintained full-entry equilibrium, the model delivers a three-region policy rule: deterministic low-item selling, a blind box with repeat purchases after either unsuccessful history, and deterministic high-item selling when comparison pressure exceeds a threshold.

The firm commits to a fixed price and a target-item probability.

Stopping purchases therefore need not eliminate social comparison loss.

This setting raises two related questions: which repeat-purchase patterns can a firm induce at a fixed price, and when does stronger comparison support probabilistic selling?

Stronger social comparison can make the target item more widely available and eventually eliminate probabilistic selling.

The result concerns the firm's response to comparison pressure; the firm does not choose consumers' comparison sensitivity.
